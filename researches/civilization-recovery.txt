
Offline Civilization Recovery Repository
Executive summary
The strongest approach is not to build one giant Git repository full of binaries, PDFs, ZIM files, and model weights. The best design is a manifest-driven Git repo that stores code, manifests, metadata schemas, chunking rules, checksums, and packaging recipes, while the heavy artifacts live outside Git in reproducible bundles on disk, release archives, or mirrored folders. For offline browsing, use Kiwix/OpenZIM and official publisher downloads; for exact search, use ripgrep over normalized text; for semantic retrieval, use a local vector store; and for question answering, run a small set of quantized local LLMs through Ollama or llama.cpp. That architecture is far more maintainable, legally safer, and easier to replicate onto ordinary Windows/Linux PCs than trying to make Git itself the artifact store. 

For your stated constraints, the best default stack is this: Kiwix/OpenZIM + official PDFs/manuals for acquisition, Markdown/JSONL/Parquet for normalized text, LanceDB as the default local vector layer, ripgrep as the exact-search fallback, Ollama as the easiest runtime, and Nomic Embed Text v1.5 as the default English embedding model. If you want the most portable runtime and fully reproducible GGUF builds, pair or replace Ollama with llama.cpp. For end-user convenience on laptops, GPT4All remains useful because it runs on ordinary desktops/laptops, integrates local document retrieval, and explicitly targets consumer hardware without requiring a GPU. 

The best model mix for a no-GPU PC is tiered by RAM. On a 16 GB RAM machine, start with Gemma 3 4B Q4_K_M or Mistral 7B Q4_K_M. On a 16–24 GB RAM machine, add Llama 3.1 8B Q4_K_M as the main general-purpose model. On a 32 GB RAM machine, add Mistral Small 24B for the highest-quality offline answering that is still plausible on a regular PC. Official Ollama pages list approximate artifact sizes of 3.3 GB for Gemma 3 4B Q4_K_M, 4.4 GB for Mistral 7B Q4_K_M, 4.9 GB for Llama 3.1 8B Q4_K_M, and 14 GB for Mistral Small 24B, which makes these practical planning anchors for disk and RAM budgeting. 

The main complexity is licensing, not software. Several of the highest-value sources for “restart civilization” knowledge are reusable but non-commercial or otherwise condition-laden. WHO and FAO publications are generally under CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO. iFixit content is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 with separate commercial/LLM licensing available. CDC material created by or for the U.S. government is generally public domain, but CDC itself notes international copyright considerations outside the United States. OpenStax is especially important to verify book by book and version by version, because surfaced official pages were inconsistent: some pages say CC BY 4.0, while other official pages say CC BY-NC-SA and explicitly disallow ingestion into LLMs without permission. That means your repo should treat licensing as first-class metadata and keep questionable or NC content in separately packageable tiers. 

A few important details were unspecified in your brief and materially affect the final build: the exact storage budget, whether commercial redistribution matters, whether you want a single external-drive image or modular bundles, and whether the repo should include only clearly open material or also non-commercial material for personal/private use. Where those details were unspecified, the recommendations below assume personal or non-commercial use, English-first content, Windows/Linux compatibility, and no GPU dependency.

Design principles and recommended architecture
A “civilization recovery” archive works best when it is built as two layers. The first layer is the authoritative source layer: raw ZIMs, PDFs, EPUBs, HTML exports, CSVs, and model files. The second layer is the retrieval layer: normalized text, chunk metadata, embeddings, full-text indexes, and local-app configuration. This separation preserves provenance, allows re-indexing without re-downloading, and makes it possible to prove what source a generated answer came from. That is especially important when the corpus mixes encyclopedic text, medical guidance, engineering manuals, and practical repair content. 

The right operational principle is browse originals, answer from normalized derivatives, always link back to source records. Kiwix is excellent for offline human browsing of large free-knowledge libraries, while vector retrieval and grep both work better against normalized Markdown/JSONL produced from those originals. Faiss, LanceDB, Qdrant, and sqlite-vec all support this pattern in different ways, but the cleanest implementation for an offline PC is to keep raw content immutable and regenerate indexes as needed.

Content acquisition and curation
The most valuable corpus is not “everything,” but a prioritized, legally usable, and operationally searchable subset. For a civilization-recovery use case, the correct priority order is usually: reference knowledge, health and sanitation, food/agriculture, repair and fabrication, science and engineering education, then literature and culture. Kiwix is particularly strong here because its official directory includes offline packages for Wikibooks, Wiktionary, Wikisource, Project Gutenberg, LibreTexts, iFixit, and many Wikimedia properties in a standardized ZIM format.

Curation criteria and metadata schema
Your curation policy should reject the instinct to “mirror everything equally.” The best filter is a weighted rubric that favors authoritativeness, practical utility under resource scarcity, reuse rights, format quality, and update stability. A chemistry handbook, a crop disease manual, a field sanitation guide, a machine repair procedure, and a basic electricity text should outrank generic blog content even if that blog is easier to scrape. Where two sources overlap, prefer the one with the clearer provenance and the more permissive license. 

A good record schema is simple enough to keep stable, but rich enough to support search, packaging, safety review, and re-indexing. The minimum useful fields are:

Field	Purpose
doc_id	Stable canonical ID
source_family	kiwix, who, fao, cdc, openstax, appropedia, ose, etc.
title	Human-readable title
source_url	Canonical source page
download_url	Exact artifact URL if applicable
license	Normalized expression plus human-readable note
version_or_snapshot	Date/version of the artifact
language	Default en for this build
domain_tags	medicine, agriculture, repair, energy, governance, etc.
doc_type	zim, pdf, epub, html, csv, gguf, etc.
publication_date	Upstream publication date when known
checksum_sha256	Integrity verification
normalized_path	Path to derived Markdown/JSONL
chunk_profile	Which chunking rule was applied
safety_class	general, medical, electrical, chemical, fabrication, etc.
attribution_text	Exact required attribution line
review_status	unreviewed, reviewed, restricted, excluded

Because rights vary so sharply across OpenStax, WHO, FAO, CDC, iFixit, Appropedia, and OSE, the fields that matter most are license, version_or_snapshot, checksum_sha256, and attribution_text. Those are not optional metadata in a project like this; they are the difference between a reproducible archive and a legal mess. 

Storage formats and retrieval architecture
The right storage pattern is keep the original, then create a normalized derivative optimized for search. For originals, use the native artifact format whenever it is already distribution-friendly: ZIM, PDF, EPUB, HTML, CSV, and GGUF. For derivatives, standardize on Markdown for prose-like documents, JSONL for document records and chunk records, and optionally Parquet for chunk tables or evaluation datasets. Pandoc is the best general converter for markup-rich formats; Apache Tika is the best general extractor/metadata tool across heterogeneous document types; and pdfminer.six is useful when you specifically need text-first PDF extraction from born-digital PDFs. 

Recommended file-format policy
Layer	Recommended format	Why
Source artifacts	*.zim, *.pdf, *.epub, *.html, *.csv, *.gguf	Best provenance and easy re-verification
Normalized documents	*.md	Human-readable, grep-friendly, easy to diff
Record store	documents.jsonl	One record per source document; simple and portable
Chunk store	chunks.jsonl or chunks.parquet	Good for embedding and rebuild pipelines
Full-text metadata	SQLite / Meilisearch / Tantivy index dir	Fast exact and BM25 retrieval
Vector index	LanceDB dir, Faiss files, Qdrant local path, or sqlite-vec SQLite DB	Depends on desired operational complexity

Source notes: Pandoc is a universal document converter; Tika extracts text and metadata from a very wide range of file types from a single interface; pdfminer.six is specifically oriented toward extracting text directly from PDF source structure. 

The best chunking policy is structure-aware and domain-aware, not a naive fixed-length splitter. Use heading-aware chunks for textbooks/manuals, preserve ordered procedures as intact units, keep tables together or convert them into row-group JSON where table semantics matter, and avoid mixing unrelated sections just to hit a token target. For English RAG on practical documents, a good default is 450–700 tokens with 60–100 tokens overlap for expository prose, 150–300 tokens for procedures/checklists, and one logical section per chunk for formulas/code/specs. That strategy is a recommendation rather than a hard vendor rule, but it fits the way embedding models and local retrieval systems are documented and used in practice. The most important thing is to retain heading path, source offsets, and parent-child relationships so the UI can show the surrounding context after retrieval. 

Vector databases and offline search
Best overall vector choice: LanceDB for a local, offline, read-mostly archive where you want one storage layer that supports vector search, full-text search, filtering, and hybrid search. LanceDB’s documentation emphasizes a disk-based indexing philosophy, supports IVF and HNSW-family indexes, offers full-text BM25 indexing, and supports hybrid search in the OSS docs. 

Best minimal-performance choice: Faiss if you are comfortable building some glue code yourself. It is extremely mature, explicitly documents index tradeoffs and bytes-per-vector formulas, and supports exact, HNSW, and IVF/PQ-style indexes. It is a library, not a batteries-included app database, so you will need to pair it with separate metadata/full-text infrastructure. 

Best local-service choice: Qdrant if you want a more database-shaped local experience with filtering and a clean API. Qdrant documents local mode, on-disk persistence, security controls, and official benchmark methodology. It is heavier operationally than LanceDB or Faiss for a single-user offline laptop, but easier to expose to multiple tools through a stable local API. 

Best tiny embedded choice: sqlite-vec only when you explicitly want an ultra-portable, no-server, SQLite-native package and can accept immaturity. Its docs say it runs anywhere and is pure SQL with no extra server, but the project also warns that it is still pre-v1 and subject to breaking changes. SQLite’s newer experimental Vec1 extension is also worth watching, but for a serious recovery archive today it is safer to treat SQLite-vector tooling as a specialized option, not the default. 

Option	Best for	Advantages	Tradeoffs	Verdict
LanceDB	Default offline RAG archive	Hybrid search, disk-based philosophy, vector + FTS + filtering in one stack	Less battle-tested in classic ANN public benchmark culture than Faiss	Best default
Faiss	Maximum performance and control	Mature, fast, explicit memory/index tradeoffs, excellent for custom pipelines	You must build metadata/full-text glue yourself	Best performance core
Qdrant local	Multi-tool local API + filtering	On-disk local mode, good filtering, official security and benchmark docs	More operational complexity	Best local service
sqlite-vec	One-file embedded deployments	No server, very portable, SQL-native	Pre-v1 and more limited for large serious builds	Best tiny option

Benchmark note: public ANN-Benchmarks pages are readily available for at least hnsw(faiss) and qdrant, and Qdrant also publishes its own open benchmark harness and methodology. For LanceDB and sqlite-vec, the safer engineering answer is to run your own acceptance benchmark on your corpus before locking the design. 

For exact and lexical search, the best answer is not a single tool but a stack. Use ripgrep for dead-simple exact search across normalized text on every machine, Meilisearch if you want a friendly local search API/UI layer with filtering and ranking, and Tantivy if you want to embed BM25/full-text search directly into a custom Rust desktop/server binary. ripgrep is explicitly line-oriented and cross-platform; Meilisearch documents full-text, filtering, and semantic/AI-oriented indexing; Tantivy is a Lucene-like Rust library with BM25, fast startup, mmap support, and a CLI/server tutorial.

Local models, embeddings, and runtimes
Embedding models and local embedding tools
For an English-first corpus on ordinary PCs, the best default embedding model is Nomic Embed Text v1.5. Its official model card specifically documents the search_document: and search_query: prefixes for RAG, and the official GGUF repository gives a compact spectrum of local files with Q4_K_M around 84 MB, Q8_0 around 146 MB, and F16 around 274 MB. That makes it unusually practical for offline builds. The main caveat is that the GGUF page notes a 2048-token default context in llama.cpp unless you use context-extension techniques; the original transformer-based implementation is the better choice if you need the full long-context behavior without that caveat. 

A very strong alternative is EmbeddingGemma. Ollama describes it as a 300M-parameter embedding model from Google, and the official Google model card emphasizes query-vs-document prompt templates for retrieval. If you expect multilingual use or want Google’s small embedding family, it is an excellent second option. For a very small fallback, all-MiniLM-L6-v2 remains practical, but its official model card says it is intended for sentences and short paragraphs and truncates input beyond 256 word pieces, which makes it less attractive for long manual-like chunks. 

In terms of tooling, the best build-time choice is SentenceTransformers for reproducible batch indexing; the best local-service choice is Ollama’s /api/embed endpoint; and the easiest desktop-centric route is GPT4All / Nomic local embeddings, which explicitly support local inference and document indexing workflows. 

Embedding model	Best use	Practical notes	Verdict
Nomic Embed Text v1.5	Default English RAG	Explicit RAG prefixes; small GGUF quantizations down to sub-100 MB	Best default
EmbeddingGemma	Small Google embedding option	Query/document prompt templates; 300M-scale family	Best alternative
all-MiniLM-L6-v2	Tiny fallback	Very light, but short-input oriented and truncates at 256 word pieces	Best tiny fallback

LLM runtimes
For runtime, there is a clear split between ease, portability, and desktop UX.

Ollama is the best overall runtime for most people. Its docs expose a stable local API, official embedding endpoints, model pulling, and a very simple operator experience. It is the easiest path to getting a local HTTP endpoint that other tools can call offline. 

llama.cpp is the best portability/runtime-engineering choice. The project requires GGUF, provides conversion scripts for non-GGUF weights, and is the cleanest route if you want to own the entire artifact pipeline from upstream Hugging Face weights to your own quantized files. It is also the most reproducible option when you want the repo to define how models are built rather than just which models are pulled. 

GPT4All is the best desktop “just run it” option for non-specialist users. Its docs say it runs privately on everyday desktops and laptops, requires no API calls or GPU, supports LocalDocs, and focuses on the 3–13B model range on consumer hardware. This makes it valuable as a packaged fallback even if Ollama or llama.cpp are your main engineering targets.

Build pipeline, repo layout, and operations
The core engineering move is to make the project manifest-first and reproducible. Every upstream item gets a manifest entry; every pipeline step is deterministic; every bundle has checksums; and every chunk ID remains stable across re-indexes unless the source actually changed. That gives you a repo that can be rebuilt, mirrored to a fresh PC, and audited when answers are wrong. Tools like Pandoc, Tika, SentenceTransformers/Ollama embeddings, LanceDB/Faiss, and local model runtimes fit naturally into this pattern.

The rule should be straightforward: store code, manifests, schemas, prompts, and tiny sample fixtures in Git; store corpora, indexes, and model weights outside Git. For most users, that means Git for orchestration, plus external SSD bundles or release archives for payloads. If you absolutely need Git-hosted binary artifacts, use it only for very small items; do not treat Git as the primary store for 100 GB–500 GB content packs or multiple multi-gigabyte models. That recommendation is architectural judgment, not a vendor rule, but it is the only approach that stays practical at civilization-archive scale.

Risks, licensing, and implementation checklist
The legal checklist is the part many technically good offline archives get wrong. The archive should store license metadata per document, preserve upstream attribution text, keep NC sources in logically separate package tiers, and record whether a source allows LLM ingestion, redistribution, commercial use, and derivatives. This is particularly important because the corpus you want spans public-domain, CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC-SA, and version-specific terms. OpenStax deserves special caution because surfaced official pages were inconsistent; WHO/FAO/iFixit are much clearer but still non-commercial; CDC content is generous but not blanket-risk-free outside the U.S.; and Project Gutenberg is public-domain-heavy but jurisdiction-sensitive. 

Security and safety matter just as much as licensing. Parser pipelines can be attacked by malformed files; Apache Tika’s security documentation explicitly discusses command execution, deserialization, and out-of-memory risks. Local services should be bound to 127.0.0.1 by default; Qdrant’s security docs explicitly recommend that for local development and note that its default binding can otherwise expose the instance more broadly than intended. Treat retrieved text as untrusted input to the model, because manuals and web-derived content can contain prompt-injection-like instructions even offline. For high-risk domains such as medicine, electricity, chemistry, and fabrication, the UI should prefer source-grounded answers with explicit citations or source excerpts, not free-form model improvisation. 

The best update strategy is slow, explicit, and reproducible. Knowledge sources such as Kiwix directories and some reference repositories update on their own cadences, while model families and embedding standards evolve separately. Do not auto-update everything blindly. Instead, freeze quarterly or semiannual snapshots, test retrieval quality and bundle integrity, and promote only verified snapshots into “stable” releases. That is less glamorous than constant freshness, but much safer for an archive whose value comes from reliability. 

Recommended next steps
The best next step is to build a balanced English-first v1 rather than an over-ambitious everything-pack. Start with Kiwix Wikibooks + Wiktionary + iFixit + selected LibreTexts, plus curated official CDC/WHO/FAO manuals and a narrow set of OpenStax titles whose rights you verify manually. Then normalize only what you need for retrieval, index with LanceDB + ripgrep, and ship Gemma 3 4B, Mistral 7B, and Llama 3.1 8B as the default model trio. That gives you a genuinely usable offline system that still fits an ordinary PC.

Open questions and limitations
A few important items remain inherently variable. I did not verify every individual OpenStax title/version because the surfaced official pages were inconsistent on license and AI-ingestion terms; you should manually verify every OpenStax title before bundling it. I also did not benchmark LanceDB, Faiss, Qdrant, and sqlite-vec on your exact final corpus, so the vector-store recommendation is based on official docs, public benchmark availability, and offline-operational fit rather than your workload-specific measurements. Finally, exact storage totals depend heavily on whether you include very large literary collections, how much raw-vs-normalized duplication you tolerate, and whether the archive must be personally usable only or legally redistributable to third parties.
